# Research Seed: The Future of Precious Metals

**A multiagent simulation of monetary trust, industrial demand, technological change and resource scarcity**

Version: 1.0  
Reference date: November 1, 2026  
Simulation horizon: 2027 to 2040  
Geographic scope: Global  
Research subjects: Gold, silver and copper  
Simulation platform: MiroFish

## 1. Research Objective

### Central research question

How will the economic, monetary, industrial and societal relevance of gold and silver evolve between 2027 and 2040, and which developments could cause these metals to gain or lose importance?

Copper serves as a comparative industrial metal to help distinguish general demand for physical resources from the monetary characteristics of precious metals.

The simulation must investigate potentially opposing developments and identify unexpected interactions among economic actors.

It must not assume that precious metals will necessarily appreciate.

### Specific research questions

1. Will gold become more important as a monetary reserve asset?
2. Will silver become increasingly important as an industrial resource?
3. Can silver's industrial and investment demand reinforce each other?
4. Could technological substitution reduce silver consumption despite expanding electrification?
5. How might artificial intelligence affect employment, purchasing power, production and resource demand?
6. Will growing sovereign debt change central bank reserve strategies?
7. Could digitalization increase demand for physical assets as a form of protection against institutional or technological dependencies?
8. Could social media accelerate speculative commodity cycles?
9. Can physical bullion markets develop persistent premiums over financial reference prices?
10. Under which conditions could gold and silver become structurally less attractive?
11. Which consequences would these developments have for the relative importance of copper?
12. Could a prolonged commodity bear market occur despite growing industrial consumption?

### Definition of relevance

Relevance must not be measured exclusively through price.

The simulation shall distinguish:

* Monetary relevance: Use as reserve assets, collateral, stores of value and monetary substitutes.
* Industrial relevance: Physical consumption, technological necessity and substitution difficulty.
* Strategic relevance: Importance for supply chain security, geopolitical leverage and national economic resilience.
* Investment relevance: Portfolio allocation, institutional ownership and private investment demand.
* Societal relevance: Public trust, perceived security, ownership preferences and cultural attitudes.
* Market relevance: Liquidity, market depth, access to physical products and price formation.

A metal can become more important industrially while simultaneously generating poor investment returns.

## 2. Initial World State

### 2.1 The global economy in 2026

The simulation begins in an environment characterized by substantial public debt, geopolitical competition, technological transformation and changing monetary conditions.

The following developments form part of the initial conditions:

**Fiscal conditions**

The IMF reported global public debt just below 94% of world GDP in 2025 and projected that it could reach approximately 100% by 2029.

Elevated debt creates possible tensions among taxation, public expenditure, inflation, interest rates and financial stability.

However, high debt does not automatically imply currency collapse, monetary financing or rising gold prices.

**Technology**

Artificial intelligence is expanding across software, research, manufacturing, logistics, finance and other economic activities.

Its effects on productivity and employment remain uncertain.

AI simultaneously creates additional demand for computing infrastructure, electricity and various industrial materials.

**Geopolitics**

Strategic competition influences resource access, international trade, industrial policy and the geographical distribution of production.

Countries may seek to reduce dependencies on foreign suppliers through strategic stockpiles, domestic capacity and diversified supply chains.

**Financial markets**

Financial markets contain institutional investors, retail participants, automated trading systems, commodity funds, derivatives and physically backed investment products.

Their behavior can produce rapid price changes that do not directly correspond to short term changes in physical consumption.

### 2.2 The role of copper

Copper provides an industrial benchmark.

It is an essential material for electrical infrastructure, manufacturing and a wide range of technological applications.

The IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 projects approximately 7 million additional tonnes of copper demand by 2040 under its stated policies scenario.

The IEA also identifies potential supply constraints under its modeled production assumptions.

These projections must remain conditional rather than being treated as guaranteed outcomes.

**Research implication:** If all three metals gain importance, a broad resource or electrification trend may explain the outcome. If gold gains while copper weakens, monetary considerations may be more important.

Sources:

https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2026/04/15/fiscal-monitor-april-2026

https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2026

## 3. Gold: Initial Market Structure

### 3.1 Fundamental characteristics

Gold is a durable, internationally recognized and relatively scarce commodity.

Its principal economic functions include investment, reserve management, jewelry, technological applications and wealth preservation.

Unlike industrial commodities that are consumed or dissipated, a substantial proportion of previously mined gold remains in accessible aboveground stocks.

The annual mining supply is therefore only one component of the broader available stock.

### 3.2 Main sources of demand

**Central banks**

Central banks may acquire or sell gold to diversify reserves, manage perceived currency risks or pursue monetary security objectives.

Their decisions depend on institutional mandates, reserve composition, geopolitical considerations and financial constraints.

**Private investors**

Private investors hold physical gold, investment products or other gold exposure for portfolio diversification, speculation and perceived financial security.

**Institutional investors**

Financial institutions respond to expected returns, portfolio risks, liquidity and the opportunity cost of holding nonyielding assets.

**Jewelry consumers**

Jewelry demand depends on purchasing power, cultural preferences and prices.

**Technology companies**

Gold is used in certain electronics and high reliability applications, but its industrial role is substantially different from that of silver and copper.

### 3.3 Relevant economic mechanisms

Gold investment demand may benefit from:

* Declining real interest rates.
* Increased monetary uncertainty.
* Financial instability.
* Rising institutional demand for diversification.
* Geopolitical uncertainty.
* Increasing central bank reserve allocations.

Gold demand may weaken under:

* Persistently high positive real interest rates.
* Improved confidence in monetary institutions.
* Strong competing asset returns.
* Declining investment demand.
* Reduced household purchasing power.
* Central bank reserve sales.

These relationships are conditional, not deterministic.

### 3.4 Supply adjustment

Gold mine production cannot immediately respond to price fluctuations.

New discoveries, permitting, financing and development may require several years.

Higher gold prices can encourage additional recycling.

Existing aboveground inventories can also be mobilized through investment sales.

### 3.5 Initial evidence

The World Gold Council's July 2026 outlook anticipated continued importance of investment and central bank demand, while noting sensitivity to real yields, monetary policy and the US dollar.

This is an institutional market outlook, not a known future outcome.

Source:

https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-demand-trends/gold-demand-trends-q2-2026/outlook

## 4. Silver: Initial Market Structure

### 4.1 Fundamental characteristics

Silver has a dual economic role.

It is both an investment precious metal and an industrial input.

Its industrial uses include electrical contacts, conductive pastes, photovoltaic systems, electronic components and other applications benefiting from its physical properties.

Its price may respond to investment demand, monetary expectations and changes in industrial activity.

### 4.2 Verified historical baseline

According to the World Silver Survey 2026:

| Indicator | 2025 |
|---|---:|
| Global mine production | 846.6 million troy ounces |
| Global total demand | 1,130.6 million troy ounces |
| Global industrial demand | 657.4 million troy ounces |
| Change in industrial demand | −3% year over year |
| Change in coin and bar demand | +14% year over year |

Industrial demand represented approximately 58.1% of total reported demand.

The report anticipates a sixth consecutive annual market deficit in 2026.

It also identifies photovoltaic silver substitution, reduced silver usage per unit of technology, recycling and price sensitive demand as important counterforces.

**Critical interpretation:** A cumulative supply deficit does not by itself establish imminent physical exhaustion. Inventory drawdowns, investment flows, market definitions and stock availability must also be considered.

Source:

https://silverinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/World-Silver-Survey-2026.pdf

### 4.3 Industrial demand channels

**Photovoltaics**

Demand depends on the number and type of solar cells produced, silver consumption per cell, technology choices and installation volumes.

Greater solar deployment does not necessarily increase total silver consumption if material intensity declines sufficiently.

**Electronics and computing**

Growing production of electronic equipment and data infrastructure may increase demand for conductive materials.

The quantity of silver required per device can change.

**Transportation**

Vehicle electrification may increase electrical material requirements, but vehicle architecture and production volumes also matter.

**Robotics and automation**

Robotic manufacturing systems require electronics, motors, connections and industrial infrastructure.

Silver may benefit from certain components, but most material demand is not necessarily silver specific.

### 4.4 Supply characteristics

Silver is frequently produced as a byproduct of mining other metals, including copper, lead, zinc and gold.

Consequently, higher silver prices may not cause a proportionate increase in mine production.

Mine operators also respond to the economics of their principal products.

Recycling supply depends on prices, collection systems, material concentrations, processing technology and economic viability.

### 4.5 Investment demand

Physical coins and bars may attract demand during periods of monetary uncertainty or speculative enthusiasm.

Financial products provide additional avenues for exposure.

Investor activity may amplify price movements independently of industrial consumption.

### 4.6 Physical bullion markets

Physical bullion products have transaction characteristics different from exchange traded silver contracts.

The final consumer price may incorporate:

* Underlying metal value.
* Manufacturing and minting costs.
* Distribution.
* Dealer margins.
* Insurance and transportation.
* Local taxation.
* Inventory scarcity.
* Collectibility and product specific demand.

Different acquisition and liquidation channels produce different prices.

Private markets may support transaction prices above dealer buyback offers, but their liquidity and execution times vary.

Physical premiums should not be treated as permanent or as proof that the international reference price is incorrect.

### 4.7 Price feedback mechanisms

Possible positive feedback:

Higher investor demand increases prices, which attracts attention and additional buyers.

Possible negative feedback:

Higher prices encourage recycling, suppress discretionary consumption and accelerate technological substitution.

The interaction between these mechanisms is a central simulation subject.

## 5. Artificial Intelligence and Resource Demand

### 5.1 Computing infrastructure

AI expansion requires computing facilities, networking equipment, power systems and cooling infrastructure.

The IEA's 2025 Energy and AI report estimated that global data center electricity demand could reach approximately 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case.

This is an energy projection, not a direct projection of silver consumption.

### 5.2 Potential resource demand effects

Expanded computing infrastructure may increase demand for electrical infrastructure, semiconductors, contacts and supporting equipment.

Copper may benefit from electricity networks and related installations.

Silver may benefit through selected electronic applications.

Gold may benefit through certain specialized electronics applications.

The magnitude of these effects cannot be inferred from data center electricity consumption alone.

### 5.3 Potential efficiency effects

AI could also reduce commodity intensity through:

* Improved material engineering.
* More efficient production.
* Optimized product designs.
* Improved recycling technologies.
* Accelerated discovery of substitutes.
* Better logistics and resource allocation.

These developments could reduce demand per unit of economic output.

### 5.4 Employment and wealth distribution

AI may increase productivity and total economic output.

However, gains may be distributed unequally.

Possible pathways include higher household incomes, labor displacement, new occupations, lower production costs and increased concentration of capital.

Each pathway implies different effects on discretionary investment demand for gold and silver.

### 5.5 The physical ownership hypothesis

Hypothesis H1:

As more economic activity becomes digital and dependent on financial or technical intermediaries, some households may assign greater value to directly controlled physical assets.

Competing hypothesis H2:

Improved digital infrastructure, financial accessibility and institutional trust could reduce demand for direct physical possession.

Neither hypothesis is an established outcome.

Source:

https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai

## 6. Money, Sovereign Debt and Institutional Trust

### 6.1 Monetary credibility

Confidence in national currencies depends on price stability, governance, fiscal sustainability and the credibility of monetary authorities.

A loss of confidence may increase demand for alternative stores of value.

However, these alternatives include foreign currencies, government securities, equities, property and other assets, not exclusively gold or silver.

### 6.2 Debt sustainability

Persistent debt accumulation may lead to some combination of fiscal adjustment, inflation, financial repression, restructuring and economic growth.

The simulation must not assume monetary expansion is the only possible response.

### 6.3 Central bank reserve management

Reserve managers balance liquidity, safety, diversification and institutional requirements.

Gold competes with foreign currency reserves and other internationally recognized reserve assets.

Silver has a substantially smaller established role in modern official reserves.

### 6.4 Digital currencies and tokenization

Digital payments, tokenized deposits, stablecoins and central bank digital currencies may change payment infrastructure.

Their development does not automatically eliminate the importance of physical reserve assets.

The simulation should investigate whether technological modernization strengthens or weakens trust in incumbent institutions.

## 7. Market Structure and Collective Behavior

### 7.1 Financial price formation

International metal prices emerge from interactions among physical transactions, derivatives, inventory financing, investment products and expectations.

Derivative markets may contribute liquidity and risk transfer.

They can also transmit leverage and amplify short term volatility.

The existence of a large derivatives market alone is not evidence of price manipulation or physical shortage.

### 7.2 Social media

Information spreads rapidly through video platforms, financial communities, investment forums and algorithmic recommendation systems.

Market narratives may become self reinforcing.

Relevant behavioral phenomena include:

* Fear of missing out.
* Momentum chasing.
* Loss aversion.
* Anchoring.
* Herding.
* Selective exposure to confirming information.
* Panic selling.
* Contrarian accumulation.

Social media can also spread useful information and expose misleading narratives.

### 7.3 Hypothesis: Accelerated commodity cycles

Increased information dissemination, digital brokerage access and automated execution could accelerate investor responses.

However, faster information transmission does not necessarily eliminate long bear markets.

Long term cycles can still result from supply investment, changes in monetary policy and persistent shifts in demand.

### 7.4 Physical and financial market divergence

Temporary divergences between physical product prices and metal reference prices may result from:

* Product shortages.
* Distribution capacity.
* Taxation.
* Manufacturing constraints.
* Speculative retail demand.
* Shipping delays.
* Dealer inventory policies.
* Collector preferences.

The simulation should distinguish persistent structural premiums from temporary commercial frictions.

Every actor must possess a consistent objective, available information, resource constraints and a decision mechanism.

Agents should not know future events.

| Actor group | Primary objective | Constraints |
|---|---|---|
| Central banks | Reserve stability and diversification | Mandates, liquidity, politics |
| Governments | Economic stability and strategic security | Fiscal capacity, elections, legislation |
| Gold miners | Sustainable profit | Reserves, costs, permitting |
| Silver miners | Sustainable profit and continued production | Byproduct dependence, capital |
| Copper miners | Industrial supply and profitability | Project development, energy |
| Refiners | Processing throughput and margins | Feedstock, capacity |
| Bullion dealers | Inventory turnover and profitability | Supply, hedging, capital |
| Industrial manufacturers | Reliable and economical inputs | Substitution, contracts |
| Solar manufacturers | Competitive technology production | Efficiency, materials, innovation |
| Technology corporations | Computing growth and profitability | Energy, capital, supply chains |
| Recycling companies | Economically viable material recovery | Collection, technology |
| Institutional investors | Risk adjusted returns | Mandates, liquidity, leverage |
| Private investors | Wealth preservation or growth | Income, emotions, information |
| Speculative traders | Trading profitability | Leverage, volatility |
| Media and influencers | Attention, audience and revenue | Competition, credibility |
| Researchers | Technology development | Funding, scientific uncertainty |
| Workers and households | Income and economic security | Employment, savings |

### 8.1 Heterogeneity

Agent groups must not behave uniformly.

For example, private investors should include:

1. Long term conservative savers.
2. Momentum investors.
3. Contrarian investors.
4. Leveraged speculators.
5. Collectors.
6. Households with limited savings.
7. Investors who prefer financial products.
8. Investors who prefer physical ownership.

Central banks should likewise have differing objectives and constraints.

### 8.2 Information limitations

Agents may receive information through:

* Public statistics.
* Market prices.
* Professional research.
* Social media.
* Industry relationships.
* Government communication.
* Their own trading and production experience.

Information should spread unevenly.

Agents may interpret identical information differently.

## 9. Economic Relationships and Feedback Loops

### Mechanism A: Monetary insecurity

Perceived fiscal or monetary deterioration may cause some investors to increase demand for gold.

Higher demand may increase prices.

Higher prices may attract speculative buying or encourage profit taking.

The final effect depends on competing behaviors.

### Mechanism B: Industrial scarcity

Higher industrial consumption combined with constrained supply may reduce available inventories.

Prices may respond upward.

Producers may react through material efficiency, substitution or recycling.

The market can eventually rebalance without requiring a collapse in final demand.

### Mechanism C: Technological substitution

Rising silver prices may incentivize research into alternative materials.

Successful substitution lowers silver intensity.

This can reduce long term demand even when the number of manufactured devices grows.

### Mechanism D: Investment enthusiasm

An accelerating price trend attracts media attention.

Retail participation increases.

Dealers experience stronger demand.

Physical premiums and delivery times may rise.

The resulting enthusiasm can strengthen temporarily and eventually reverse.

### Mechanism E: Financial stress

During a liquidity crisis, investors may sell liquid assets to obtain cash.

Gold and silver can decline alongside equities.

Once forced selling decreases, prices may recover, remain depressed or decline further depending on underlying conditions.

### Mechanism F: AI productivity expansion

AI improves productivity.

Production and infrastructure requirements change.

Household incomes, energy consumption and commodity intensity respond in different directions.

The resulting net demand for metals is uncertain.

## 10. Competing Future Scenarios

The simulation must investigate all scenarios without assigning preferred outcomes.

### Scenario A: Stable Digital Prosperity

**Initial assumptions**

Productivity increases through AI.

Inflation and public finances remain manageable.

Financial institutions retain public trust.

Material efficiency improves.

**Questions**

Does higher industrial production outweigh efficiency gains?

Does gold lose relative investment importance?

Does silver retain its industrial relevance without achieving strong investment returns?

Does copper become the primary beneficiary of electrification?

### Scenario B: Monetary Fragmentation

**Initial assumptions**

Public debt concerns intensify.

Geopolitical competition increases.

Some countries seek to diversify reserve assets.

Crossborder financial cooperation weakens.

**Questions**

Does demand for gold rise significantly?

Does silver benefit from investor behavior despite its limited official reserve function?

Do exchange rates and interest rates offset the positive effects?

Does geopolitical fragmentation reduce industrial activity?

### Scenario C: Resource Intensive AI Expansion

**Initial assumptions**

AI investment and automation accelerate.

Computing infrastructure expands.

Electricity demand grows.

Supply chains experience capacity constraints.

**Questions**

Which metals become critical bottlenecks?

Does silver consumption grow faster than silver saving technologies improve?

Do copper shortages constrain infrastructure expansion?

Can new technologies reduce material intensity?

### Scenario D: Commodity Demand Disappointment

**Initial assumptions**

Global growth slows.

Investment in infrastructure weakens.

Industrial efficiency improves rapidly.

Real interest rates remain relatively attractive.

**Questions**

Can silver enter a prolonged bear market despite its industrial usefulness?

Does gold retain monetary demand?

How do miners and refiners respond?

Can investors maintain demand for physical bullion during falling prices?

### Scenario E: Socially Amplified Speculative Cycles

**Initial assumptions**

Retail investor participation grows.

Social media accelerates narrative propagation.

Rapid capital flows increasingly influence commodity markets.

**Questions**

Do speculative price cycles become shorter?

Do physical product premiums respond differently from financial prices?

Does higher volatility produce stronger contrarian investment behavior?

Can speculative participation increase without sustained price appreciation?

## 11. Simulation Timeline

### Phase I: 2027 to 2030

Investigate immediate responses to AI infrastructure expansion, public debt, industrial investment and monetary policy.

Observe emerging changes in demand, investor behavior and physical market conditions.

### Phase II: 2031 to 2035

Investigate delayed effects of mining projects, infrastructure investment, technological substitution, recycling and changes in the labor market.

### Phase III: 2036 to 2040

Investigate whether structural trends persist, reverse or produce new equilibria.

Examine the relative monetary and industrial importance of each metal.

**Important limitation:** MiroFish agent interactions do not automatically correspond to calendar years. The simulation operator must explicitly define a mapping between simulated periods and years. Otherwise, the generated narrative must not be presented as a dated forecast.

Describe major decisions taken by central banks, investors, miners, manufacturers, governments and households.

### 12.3 Causal pathways

Explain the interactions producing each outcome.

Separate initial assumptions from new developments occurring during the simulation.

### 12.4 Unexpected developments

Identify developments that were not explicitly assumed in the scenario.

Explain the mechanism responsible.

### 12.5 Contradictions

Identify outcomes that violate the initial economic assumptions, known technological constraints or available evidence.

Describe alternative pathways and unresolved questions.

Avoid assigning precise forecast probabilities without a validated calibration method.

If prices are generated, treat them as hypothetical simulation outputs.

### 13.2 Comparative assessment

For every indicator, distinguish:

* Observed historical data.
* Forecast from an external institution.
* Scenario assumption.
* Emergent simulation result.
* Unsupported claim.

### 13.3 Robustness

Run each scenario multiple times using identical initial conditions but different random seeds where technically supported.

Compare results.

A mechanism that appears only once should be treated as less robust than one recurring across multiple runs.

Repeated output is not proof that the mechanism will occur in reality.

### 13.4 Counterfactual testing

Change one assumption at a time where possible.

Examples:

* Faster photovoltaic silver substitution.
* Reduced central bank gold demand.
* Higher real interest rates.
* A sustained decline in AI infrastructure investment.
* Faster recycling technology.
* Reduced retail interest in physical bullion.

Investigate which outcomes change.

## 14. Explicit Simulation Rules

1. Do not assume the investor thesis favoring precious metals is correct.
2. Do not assume monetary instability inevitably produces higher gold prices.
3. Do not assume industrial shortages inevitably generate rising prices.
4. Distinguish commodity demand from investment return.
5. Distinguish physical scarcity from dealer inventory shortages.
6. Distinguish physical bullion premiums from spot prices.
7. Do not treat social media popularity as fundamental value.
8. Do not assume faster information transmission eliminates long market cycles.
9. Do not treat all investors, governments or manufacturers as homogeneous.
10. Do not grant simulated agents knowledge of future events.
11. Distinguish production capacity from economically recoverable reserves.
12. Model technological substitution and demand destruction as possible responses to high prices.
13. Avoid deterministic claims about artificial intelligence and employment.
14. Respect material balance constraints where numerical commodity flows are modeled.
15. Clearly report missing data or insufficiently specified relationships.
16. Do not invent historical observations.
17. Do not treat a scenario as a probability forecast.
18. Identify both arguments supporting and contradicting the initial thesis.

## 15. Research Hypotheses

**H1: Physical ownership**

Increasing economic digitalization may increase the preference of certain investors for tangible assets.

**H2: Industrial silver demand**

Electrification and computing expansion may increase silver consumption, subject to technological efficiency gains.

**H3: Monetary diversification**

Persistent geopolitical and fiscal uncertainty may increase international demand for gold reserves.

**H4: Commodity substitution**

High prices may accelerate technologies that reduce the amount of silver required per unit of industrial production.

**H5: Accelerated speculation**

Digital market access and social media may increase the speed and magnitude of speculative investment cycles.

**H6: Persistent physical premiums**

Temporary bullion shortages may create retail prices that respond less rapidly than international reference prices.

**H7: AI productivity**

A sustained productivity expansion may reduce inflationary pressure and commodity intensity even while total industrial output rises.

**H8: Financial stability**

Greater institutional credibility and attractive real yields may reduce investment demand for precious metals.

**H9: Copper comparison**

Copper may benefit more directly than gold from increased electricity infrastructure investment.

**H10: Disconnected relevance and returns**

Gold and silver may become more economically important while delivering weak or negative real investment returns over extended periods.

## 17. Final Research Mandate

The purpose of this simulation is not to establish whether investing in gold or silver is profitable.

Its purpose is to investigate the future role of physical precious metals within an economy undergoing technological, monetary and geopolitical transformation.

The simulation shall identify mechanisms through which precious metals may become more important, less important or important in fundamentally different ways.

Special attention must be given to developments that contradict prevailing expectations.

The most valuable outcome is a new, coherent and potentially testable hypothesis, not confirmation of an existing investment narrative.
